# Extraction — "article 1"

**Citation:** Carpenter, J. K., Andrews, L. A., Witcraft, S. M., Powers, M. B., Smits, J. A. J., & Hofmann, S. G. (2018). Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Anxiety and Related Disorders: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trials. *Depression and Anxiety*, 35(6), 502–514. doi:10.1002/da.22728. (Read from the HHS Public Access author manuscript, 17 journal pages; page references below use the manuscript's "Carpenter et al. Page N" numbering.)

Facts only. No interpretation, no appraisal.

## Study design

- Meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials (pill or psychological placebo) of CBT for anxiety and related disorders (p.1, Abstract; pp.3–4).
- Registered with PROSPERO (CRD42016050841); conducted following PRISMA guidelines (p.4, Method).
- Update of Hofmann & Smits (2008), the only prior meta-analysis restricted to placebo-controlled CBT trials for these disorders (p.3).
- Search: PubMed and PsycINFO from first available date to 4 January 2017, plus manual searches of reference lists; 3,215 unique records, of which the first 1,165 had been screened for Hofmann & Smits (2008) under the same criteria (p.4).
- Inclusion criteria: adults 18–65; DSM-III-R, DSM-IV, or DSM-5 diagnosis of acute stress disorder (ASD), GAD, OCD, PTSD, SAD, or specific phobia by a structured, psychometrically sound diagnostic instrument; random assignment to CBT or placebo (pill or psychological); psychometrically sound severity measures; sufficient data to calculate effect sizes (p.4).
- Exclusion criteria: secondary analyses; placebos containing active ingredients (e.g., self-guided exposure); "third-wave" interventions (ACT, mindfulness-based, etc.); samples defined by a particular comorbidity; remotely delivered interventions (phone/computer) (pp.4–5).
- Psychological placebo defined as controlling for nonspecific factors (equivalent therapist contact, credible rationale, discussion of the problem) with no treatment elements of demonstrated efficacy for the target condition (p.4).
- Risk of bias assessed with the Cochrane Collaboration tool across five domains; two independent raters; inter-rater reliability for total bias score Cronbach's α = .81 (p.5).
- Random-effects model throughout; Hedges' g with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes (multiple measures within an outcome category averaged); odds ratios (Cox–Hinkley–Miettinen–Nurminen method) for categorical outcomes; heterogeneity via Q and I²; moderators via Q_B and meta-regression; publication bias via funnel plot, Egger's regression intercept, and Trim and Fill; software: Comprehensive Meta-Analysis v3 (pp.5–6).

## Sample

- 41 studies, N = 2,835 patients randomized to CBT or placebo; 16 studies new since Hofmann & Smits (2008) (p.6).
- By disorder: PTSD 14 studies (1,252 patients); SAD 12 (753); PD 5 (328); OCD 4 (313); ASD 4 (132); GAD 2 (57). No specific phobia trials appear in the final set (p.6).
- 40 studies reported continuous target-disorder outcomes; 33 reported categorical response-rate data (p.6).
- Average study sample: mean age 36.0 years (SD 6.53); 58.9% female (SD 21.21); 73.0% white (SD 21.87) (p.6).
- Medication: of 35 studies reporting on psychiatric medication, 18 excluded medicated patients, 15 included patients on stable doses, 2 excluded only specific drugs; in the 9 studies reporting counts, on average 34.2% (SD 17.4) of the sample took psychiatric medication (p.6).
- Setting: individual trials in research/clinical settings across countries (individual study settings not tabulated in main text; study-level characteristics in Supplementary Table 1, p.7).
- Attrition (dropout): weighted mean dropout 24.0% in CBT vs 17.2% in placebo; OR = 1.35 (95% CI 1.05–1.77, p < .05). Driven by the 14 PTSD studies (29.0% CBT vs 17.2% placebo; OR = 1.82, 95% CI 1.32–2.52). With PTSD studies removed: CBT 19.6% vs placebo 17.2%, OR = 1.10 (95% CI 0.78–1.55, n.s.) (p.7).
- Analysis type: 16 studies used intent-to-treat (ITT) analyses for continuous outcomes; 25 presented completer data only (p.7).
- Risk of bias: all but one study rated "unclear" in ≥1 of 5 domains; 14 of 40 had ≥1 "high risk" domain. Domain counts (low/unclear/high): sequence generation 15/26/0; allocation concealment 14/27/0; blinding 27/11/3; incomplete outcome data 9/19/13; selective outcome reporting 37/4/0 (pp.6–7).

## Measures used

- Continuous: target-disorder-specific symptom measures; other anxiety symptoms; depression; quality of life (QOL) (p.5).
- Categorical: treatment response (most conservative index selected where multiple reported) and dropout (p.5).
- Outcome instruments varied by trial (examples listed in Figure 3 caption: CAPS, CIDI PTSD module, CGI-S/CGI-I, PCL, LSAS-SR, SIAS, FNE, etc.) (Figs. 2–3 captions, pp.19–21).
- Timepoints: post-treatment and, where reported, ~6-month follow-up (mean follow-up 5.55 months, SD 2.37; 22 of 41 studies) (pp.5, 8).

## Comparator / control condition

- Psychological placebo in 29 studies (supportive counseling most common) or pill placebo (p.6).
- CBT formats: individual in 34 studies, group in 7; mean 11.0 sessions (SD 4.72). Technique mix: exposure + cognitive n = 24; primarily exposure n = 10; primarily cognitive n = 7 (p.6).

## Findings (effect sizes; 95% CIs as reported)

Post-treatment (CBT vs placebo):
- Target disorder symptoms: Hedges' g = 0.56 (0.44–0.69, p < .0001) (p.7).
- ITT samples g = 0.40 (0.25–0.56) vs completer samples g = 0.70 (0.54–0.86); difference significant (Q_B = 6.85, df = 1, p < .01) (p.7).
- Treatment response: OR = 2.97 (2.15–4.10, p < .0001); completer OR = 3.69 (2.59–5.26) vs ITT OR = 2.42 (1.65–3.54); difference not significant (Q_B = 2.56, n.s.) (p.7).
- Other anxiety symptoms (26 studies): g = 0.38 (0.25–0.51) (p.7).
- Depression (26 studies): g = 0.31 (0.21–0.41) (p.7).
- Quality of life (15 studies): g = 0.30 (0.13–0.48, p < .001) (p.7).
- Heterogeneity: target-disorder I² = 54.43%; QOL I² = 51.31%; other anxiety I² = 27.80%; depression I² = 0.00%; categorical outcomes I² = 56.32% (p.7).

Follow-up (mean 5.55 months; 22 studies):
- Target disorder symptoms: g = 0.47 (0.30–0.64).
- Other anxiety: g = 0.42 (0.23–0.62).
- Depression (14 studies): g = 0.29 (0.16–0.42).
- QOL (8 studies, all but one PTSD): g = 0.15 (0.01–0.28, p < .05) (p.8).

Publication bias:
- Egger's intercept 2.25 (p < .001), indicating funnel-plot asymmetry; Trim and Fill: 14 studies trimmed, adjusted target-disorder g = 0.33 (0.19–0.48) (p.8).

By disorder (target-disorder symptoms; significant between-disorder difference, Q_B = 12.52, df = 5, p < .05):
- OCD g = 1.13 (0.58–1.68); GAD g = 1.01 (0.44–1.57); ASD g = 0.87 (0.32–1.41); PTSD g = 0.48 (0.26–0.71); SAD g = 0.41 (0.25–0.57); PD g = 0.39 (0.12–0.65) (p.8).
- Pairwise: OCD > PTSD, SAD, PD (all p < .05); GAD > PTSD (p < .05) (p.8).
- Placebo-arm pre-post effect sizes did not differ across disorders (Q_B = 3.34, n.s.; range g = 0.38–0.55), including when pill and psychological placebo were examined separately (p.8).
- Categorical outcomes also differed by disorder (Q_B = 13.16, p < .05); OCD ORs > PTSD (p < .01) and PD (p < .05) (p.8).

Moderators (target-disorder outcomes):
- Individual CBT g = 0.54 (0.40–0.68) vs group CBT g = 0.16 (0.01–0.31); Q_B = 13.30, p < .001 (data available only in SAD and PTSD studies) (pp.8–9).
- Primarily exposure g = 0.78 (0.45–1.10) vs combined cognitive+exposure g = 0.53 (0.36–0.70) vs primarily cognitive g = 0.38 (0.18–0.57); difference not significant (Q_B = 4.39, n.s.) (p.9).
- Psychological placebo comparisons g = 0.52 vs pill placebo g = 0.66; n.s. (p.9).
- Self-report g = 0.53 vs clinician-administered g = 0.50; n.s. (p.9).
- Not significant as moderators: race, sex, sample size, medication inclusion, number of sessions, study year, risk-of-bias score (p.9).

## Limitations stated by the authors (pp.11–12)

1. Only 16 of 41 studies used ITT analyses; completer analyses appeared to yield larger effect estimates; many studies handled missing data improperly or reported it inadequately.
2. Results may have been influenced by publication bias.
3. Criteria for "treatment response" varied between studies, potentially adding heterogeneity to categorical outcomes.
4. Apart from PTSD studies, samples were not racially diverse: non-PTSD study samples averaged 82% white; only two non-PTSD studies had >23% non-white participants; generalizability to more diverse samples untested.
5. Heterogeneity in placebo conditions limits precision: pill placebo does not control for clinician contact time and may attract samples open to medication; in psychological-placebo trials, providers and possibly patients may know they are in the control condition; variability existed in the form/content of psychological placebos.
6. (Noted as future direction) No comparison against minimally instructed self-guided CBT.
