## Summary

Meta-analysis of 41 RCTs (N = 2,835; search to Jan 2017; PROSPERO-registered) comparing CBT with pill or psychological placebo for acute stress disorder, GAD, OCD, panic disorder, PTSD and social anxiety disorder. Headline result: Hedges' g = 0.56 (95% CI 0.44–0.69) on target-disorder symptoms, response OR = 2.97 (2.15–4.10), with effects persisting at ~6-month follow-up (g = 0.47).

The critical reading is that **the paper's own sensitivity analyses point to a smaller specific effect than the headline**: ITT-only studies give g = 0.40 (vs completer-only 0.70, a significant difference; only 16/41 studies were ITT), and Trim-and-Fill publication-bias correction gives g = 0.33 (0.19–0.48) after a significant Egger's test — and these two inflations stack rather than being alternatives **[inference]**. A defensible one-sentence verdict: *CBT shows a real but probably small-to-moderate specific effect over credible placebo (best estimate g ≈ 0.3–0.4), from completer-dominated, bias-affected, demographically narrow efficacy trials of mostly individual protocol CBT.* The same research group's 2023 update found g = 0.24 in post-2017 trials, which is convergent with the corrected, not the headline, figure.

Clinical translation **[inference — the trials did not use GAD-7/PHQ-9; standard conversions]**: g ≈ 0.33–0.56 corresponds to roughly 1.5–3 points on a GAD-7-like scale of advantage over an *active attention placebo* (not over no treatment), and an NNT of roughly 4–6 (≈3 at the headline figure, ≈11 for group CBT). Small incremental benefit over structured supportive contact — real, durable, and worth having, but not the "large effects" of waitlist-controlled rhetoric.

## What it supports

Citable with page refs; the load-bearing caveat is attached to each claim rather than quarantined.

1. **CBT beats placebo for anxiety-related disorders** — g = 0.56 (0.44–0.69) raw, **g = 0.40 ITT-only / g = 0.33 bias-adjusted**; response OR = 2.97 (2.15–4.10) (pp.7–8). Any essay citation of the headline figure should carry the adjusted range in the same sentence.
2. **Effects extend beyond the target disorder**: other anxiety g = 0.38 (0.25–0.51); depression g = 0.31 (0.21–0.41); quality of life g = 0.30 (0.13–0.48) (p.7).
3. **Gains hold at ~6-month follow-up** (22/41 studies; mean 5.55 months): target symptoms g = 0.47 (0.30–0.64); depression g = 0.29. A genuine design advantage: waitlist trials cannot ethically produce controlled follow-up data (pp.8–10). Caveat: follow-up reporting is itself selective (22/41 studies) **[inference]**, and QOL at follow-up is minimal (g = 0.15, mostly PTSD studies, p.8).
4. **Individual CBT outperformed group CBT** (SAD/PTSD data only): g = 0.54 vs 0.16 (0.01–0.31), Q_B = 13.30, p < .001 (pp.8–9). The group-CBT estimate is near-null against placebo — directly relevant to group provision in NHS Talking Therapies.
5. **PTSD trials show markedly higher CBT dropout**: 29.0% vs 17.2% placebo, OR = 1.82 (1.32–2.52); non-significant with PTSD removed (OR = 1.10) (p.7). Clinically: anticipate and actively manage dropout in PTSD work; methodologically: the authors themselves say the PTSD effect "may be inflated by lack of data from dropouts" (p.10).
6. **Effects differ by disorder** (Q_B = 12.52, p < .05): OCD g = 1.13, GAD g = 1.01 **(unreliable — k = 2 trials, N = 57)**, ASD g = 0.87 (k = 4), PTSD g = 0.48, SAD g = 0.41, PD g = 0.39 (p.8). The authors flag the small per-disorder study counts for caution (p.11). For the most common NHS TT presentation (GAD), this paper is close to uninformative **[inference]**.
7. **Nonspecific factors matter and are part of what patients receive**: placebo-controlled g = 0.56 vs Bandelow et al.'s (2015) waitlist-controlled g = 1.23; the authors argue the placebo-controlled figure is "likely to be the more accurate reflection of the specific effect of CBT" (p.11). A substantial share of improvement relative to no treatment is therefore attributable to expectancy, contact and natural course **[inference — the paper never partitions proportions; the two figures come from different study pools]**.
8. **Exposure-leaning trend (non-significant)**: primarily exposure g = 0.78 vs combined g = 0.53 vs primarily cognitive g = 0.38; Q_B = 4.39, n.s. (p.9). Legitimate use: supports not letting cognitive work crowd out exposure; it does not license stronger claims, and the abstract's highlighting of this null result is itself critiquable.

## What it does not support

- **"CBT is as effective as medication."** The paper's parity suggestion (p.11) is an informal juxtaposition of its pooled g = 0.56 with Bandelow's medication range (0.17–0.96, M = 0.58) — different meta-analyses, different disorder mixes (Bandelow: GAD/PD/SAD, precisely where Carpenter's CBT effects are smallest apart from k=2 GAD), no head-to-head test. Using the bias-adjusted g = 0.33 the parity claim collapses **[inference]**. Do not cite as a finding of this paper.
- **Direct applicability to NHS Talking Therapies.** The evidence base is protocol individual CBT (34/41 studies; mean 11.0 sessions) in selected research volunteers: 73% white overall, 82% white in non-PTSD samples, mean age 36, adults 18–65 only, comorbidity-defined samples excluded, remote/digital delivery excluded, 18/35 reporting studies excluded medicated patients (pp.4–6, 11). It does not estimate the effect of CBT as typically delivered in NHS TT (remote, guided self-help, group, comorbid, medicated, 6–8-session pathways) **[inference]** — and the one directly TT-relevant data point (group CBT g = 0.16) is discouraging.
- **Anything about**: third-wave therapies, internet/phone-delivered CBT, specific phobia (no trials survived selection despite being in the search), children/adolescents or older adults, session-number effects (n.s. moderator), medication co-prescription decisions (n.s.), or choice of self-report vs clinician measures (n.s.).
- **A large specific effect.** The paper's conclusion that effects "meaningfully exceed" placebo (p.12) sits uneasily with its own ITT (0.40), Trim-and-Fill (0.33) and group-CBT (0.16) figures.

## Methodological weaknesses

1. **Completer-dominated evidence.** 25/41 studies analysed completers only; completer analyses yielded significantly larger effects (0.70 vs 0.40, Q_B = 6.85, p < .01) and CBT arms lost more patients than placebo arms (OR = 1.35), so the pooled headline systematically overstates the ITT effect (p.7, p.11).
2. **Publication bias formally detected, headline unchanged.** Egger's intercept 2.25 (p < .001); Trim-and-Fill removes 14 studies and nearly halves the effect to 0.33 — yet 0.56 leads the abstract and the adjusted figure appears only in Results (p.8), with a single discussion sentence (p.11). The paper's selective emphasis is itself a critique point.
3. **"Placebo-controlled" is a category label, not an achieved blind.** Psychological placebos are structurally unblindable — providers "and even patients" may know their assignment (pp.11–12) — and are *designed* to be inert (no efficacious elements permitted, p.4), which can depress the control arm and inflate CBT's apparent specific effect; conversely a credible placebo delivers real common-factor benefit, so the subtraction can also understate CBT's value over no structured contact. The bias cuts both ways and the estimate has soft edges **[inference]**. Pill vs psychological placebo showed no significant difference (0.66 vs 0.52) despite controlling for different things — weak power or an unstable construct **[inference]**.
4. **Weak internal validity of primary studies.** All but one study "unclear" in ≥1 Cochrane domain; 14/40 with ≥1 high-risk domain; incomplete outcome data worst (9 low / 19 unclear / 13 high); sequence generation and allocation concealment mostly unclear (pp.6–7). The null risk-of-bias moderator (p.9) is weak reassurance when most studies cluster at "unclear".
5. **Underpowered subgroups.** GAD k = 2 (N = 57), OCD k = 4, ASD k = 4; group-vs-individual confounded with disorder (SAD/PTSD only); moderate unexplained heterogeneity (target-disorder I² = 54%); varying response criteria across trials (pp.6–8, 11).
6. **Analytic discretion.** Averaging multiple outcome measures per category; "most conservative" response index chosen when several were reported (p.5).
7. **Allegiance.** The senior authors are updating their own 2008 meta-analysis, and disclosed advisory relationships include digital-CBT industry roles (p.1, p.3). PROSPERO registration, dual independent extraction and rating (α = .81) are genuine safeguards — but the "meaningfully exceed placebo" gloss is allegiance-consistent where the sensitivity analyses say "small, bias-affected" **[inference]**.
8. **Dated, mixed evidence base.** Search closed January 2017; 25/41 trials predate the 2008 review; diagnostic criteria span DSM-III-R to DSM-5, and the pooled "anxiety" estimate mixes diagnostic families DSM-5 no longer groups together (pp.3–4).

## Currency status (as of July 2026)

- **Directly updated by the same group**: Bhattacharya, Goicoechea, Heshmati, Carpenter & Hofmann (2023), *Current Psychiatry Reports* 25:19–30 — placebo-controlled trials published since 2017 (10 studies, N = 1,250): target-disorder **g = 0.24**; depression g = 0.15 (n.s.); 7/10 studies were PTSD, PTSD-only g = 0.14. The most current placebo-controlled figures are markedly smaller than Carpenter's headline and consistent with its own corrected estimates. *(Sourced from abstract; full text unretrievable this session.)*
- **Long-term outcomes refined**: van Dis et al. (2020), *JAMA Psychiatry* 77(3):265–273 (69 RCTs) — effects vs control persist to 12 months across disorders; beyond 12 months sustained for GAD, SAD, PTSD but **not panic disorder**.
- **PTSD dropout direction replicated**: Lewis, Roberts, Gibson & Bisson (2020), *Eur J Psychotraumatology* — trauma-focused therapies show significantly greater dropout.
- **CBT-as-first-line repeatedly reaffirmed** in network meta-analyses: Papola et al. 2022 (*Br J Psychiatry*, panic) and Papola et al. 2023 (*JAMA Psychiatry*, GAD; corrected 2024 after data-extraction errors — a caution about meta-analytic fragility in this literature). Cuijpers et al. (2025), *JAMA Psychiatry* unified series (375 trials, N = 32,968): CBT vs *all* controls g ≈ 0.5–1.0 for panic/SAD/GAD — larger because controls include waitlist/TAU, confirming Carpenter's central point that comparator choice drives the number.
- **Field position**: the qualitative conclusion (CBT efficacious, first-line) stands unchallenged; the quantitative specific effect vs placebo has drifted downward in newer trials; attention has moved to comparative efficacy, formats, long-term durability, and declining effect sizes over time (e.g. a 2025 *Clinical Psychology Review* analysis of 30-year effect-size trends).
- **Vault consistency**: no contradictions with existing vault content (all current skill-graph nodes are depression-scoped; verified across every content file). Four mild tensions noted: (1) porting the vault's cognitive-first framing into future anxiety nodes would sit awkwardly with the paper's exposure-leaning trend; (2) vault's 8-session Brief CBT framing vs the trials' mean 11 sessions; (3) README marketing tone vs appraisal caution (product claims, not efficacy claims — tonal only); (4) the planned `GAD7_scoring` node already covers a lens suggestion.
